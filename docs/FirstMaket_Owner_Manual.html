1 What FirstMaket is

The business in one page.

FirstMaket is a marketplace. Vendors list goods, customers buy them, and FirstMaket handles the delivery — so the vendor never learns who the customer is or where they live.

What makes it different from an ordinary marketplace is the second way to buy. A customer who cannot pay ₦300,000 today can start a Pay Small Small plan: the price is frozen at that moment, and they pay it off in instalments they choose. When the plan is covered, the goods are delivered. Prices can rise in the market; the plan's price cannot.

That idea is not new in Nigeria — it is how ajo and esusu have always worked. What is new is doing it with a real marketplace behind it, and doing it in a way that never turns into lending, deposit-taking, or a wallet somebody could run away with.

Who uses it

AudienceWhere they workWhat they do
CustomersThe storefrontBrowse, buy outright, or save towards a purchase
VendorsVendor CenterList products, fulfil orders, get paid
StaffAdmin workspaceApprove, support, dispatch, reconcile

Each lives on its own web address, so a customer can never wander into the admin workspace by accident and a vendor session cannot reach staff tools.

2 The three guarantees

Almost everything unusual in how this software is built exists to defend one of these three promises. They are worth understanding, because they are what make FirstMaket safe to operate.

1

There is no wallet, and no balance

Money entering FirstMaket is always attached to one specific plan or one specific order, and it can only ever become those goods. There is no stored balance, and no route anywhere in the system that pays cash back out. This is the difference between a marketplace and a financial institution — and it is enforced by the software, not by policy.

2

Only a verified payment moves money

A customer returning from the payment page proves nothing — that page can be faked. Money is only ever credited when Paystack itself confirms it, either by a signed notification or by us asking Paystack directly. Nothing a customer's browser sends can credit an account.

3

Vendors never see customer identity

A vendor sees the product sold and an order number. The customer's name, phone number and address belong to logistics and never reach the seller. This is checked by automated tests, so a future change cannot quietly undo it.

If you remember one thing

Money in FirstMaket can only ever turn into goods. It cannot be withdrawn, transferred to another person, or held as a balance. Any future feature that would change that is a fundamentally different product with fundamentally different regulation.

3 How money moves

Three ways to pay, and what happens behind each.

Pay at once

The customer fills a basket — guests can too, and their basket follows them into their account when they sign in. At checkout the basket, its prices and the delivery address are frozen, so nothing can change underneath the customer while they are on the payment page.

Once Paystack confirms the payment, the frozen basket becomes orders, and one parcel is created per vendor. If something sold out while the customer was paying, that item is skipped and the rest of the basket still completes — they are told exactly what was left out.

Pay Small Small

The customer picks how long they want and how often they will pay. The price is frozen immediately. Every instalment is its own confirmed payment. When the total is covered, the plan becomes an order and the goods are delivered.

Life happens, so a plan can be:

  • Paused — reminders stop for a while; the frozen price holds.
  • Rescheduled — spread over longer, smaller instalments.
  • Switched — pointed at a different product, keeping everything already paid.

None of those move money. Nothing is ever forfeited.

Pay on delivery

The delivery fee is charged upfront and the goods are paid for at the door — cash to the courier, or online from the order page. The fee is taken in advance deliberately: a refused delivery costs FirstMaket real money, and that risk should sit with the person who ordered it.

5 Saving together

Three ways to save with other people — each designed so that no shared pot of money is ever created.

Group purchase

Several people fund one plan owned by one organiser — a fridge for a flat, a generator for a shop. Every contribution is recorded against the person who made it and tied to the actual payment it came from, so a share is never just a number somebody typed. The goods are delivered to the organiser, and every member is told that before they can contribute anything.

Tell members this plainly

Contributions cannot be paid back out. Someone who leaves a group keeps their name on the record of what they put in, but cannot take money out — there is no withdrawal anywhere in FirstMaket, and a group is not an exception to that.

Family group

A read-only summary of how each member's own separate plans are going. It moves no money and has no way to. Members opt in, can stop sharing at any time, and the summary never reveals what anybody is buying — only how far along they are.

Cooperative — ajo, without the cash

A rotating group. Each turn, every member contributes, and it all goes to one member — but into that member's own savings plan, not into their hand.

Where this differs from a traditional ajo

Nobody receives cash. When your turn comes, everyone's contribution brings your goods closer. If a person's reason for joining an ajo is to raise emergency cash, this is the wrong tool — and the app says so before they join, rather than letting them find out at their turn.

The rotation order is fixed the moment the group starts and cannot be changed afterwards, so an organiser can never move themselves up the queue once people have started paying in.

6 The savings assistant

Answers about a customer's own money, that they can check.

Customers can ask questions in plain language — “Am I behind?”, “How much should I be paying?”, “When will I finish?” — and get an answer worked out from their own payment history, showing the figures it used.

It is not an AI chatbot, and that is deliberate

The assistant runs on a deterministic engine: arithmetic on the customer's own records. For the job of explaining somebody's savings back to them, that beats a language model on every measure that matters here — it cannot invent a figure, every sentence traces back to a real record, it costs nothing per question, and no customer's financial data ever leaves the platform.

The software is built so an AI model can be plugged in later if there is ever a reason to prefer one. The spending controls are already in place for that day.

Two hard limits

It can suggest; it cannot act. A suggestion does nothing at all until the customer explicitly accepts it, and that acceptance is recorded separately. There is no path from “the assistant suggested it” to “it happened”.

It sees one customer. Every answer is built only from the asking customer's own records. It cannot reach anybody else's.

Even after a customer accepts a suggestion, the most it will ever do is pause a plan — something they can already do themselves. It never takes a payment.

7 Selling on FirstMaket

How a vendor joins, lists, and gets paid.

A vendor registers with their CAC business registration document, which is stored privately and reviewed by staff before they can sell anything. Nothing they list is public until a staff member approves it.

StageWhat happens
ApplyBusiness details and CAC document; reviewed by staff
ListProducts with images, category-specific fields, optional video
ApprovalEach listing reviewed; automated checks flag odd pricing for a human
FulfilConfirm stock, mark ready; FirstMaket collects and delivers
EarnCommission deducted automatically; earnings ledger per vendor
Get paidWeekly payout batches, Finance-approved, to a verified bank account

Vendors are rated on their actual fulfilment record — how often they confirm stock, how often they reject orders, how fast they prepare — and that rating drives their tier. It is recalculated nightly from stored facts, so it is always explainable.

Privacy by construction

A vendor's order screen shows the product and the order number. It has no access to the customer's name, phone number or address, and automated tests would fail if a future change exposed them.

8 The partner programme

Paying people to bring customers, without being gamed.

Approved partners get named campaign links — one per campaign, so they can tell which post actually worked. Each link is cryptographically signed, so a hand-edited web address fails rather than quietly crediting the wrong partner.

RuleWhy it exists
First link wins, permanentlyA later click can never steal a customer another partner brought in
Referrals expireA click today should not earn commission on an order two years from now
Tiers are earnedRates come from the partner's own record, so nobody negotiates a rate with support
Only deliveries paySignups are tracked but not paid — paying per signup is what makes a scheme worth gaming
Suspicion flags, never blocksA flagged conversion waits for a human; automatic blocking would let rivals knock partners offline
Finance approves payoutsMonthly, above a minimum, to a verified account only

Reviewing partners' work and paying them out are separate permissions, so one person cannot both approve a conversion and send the money for it.

Nothing on the home page is hardcoded

Every banner, deal and section on the storefront home page is set by staff, not by a developer. Discount figures are never typed in by hand — a banner either calculates a real figure from live products, or hides itself. That rule exists so the shop can never advertise a discount that does not exist.

Settings, not code changes

Operational numbers — how long a return window lasts, how many missed payments before a plan is considered dormant, how many tiles appear in a home page section, the minimum partner payout — are all settings with admin screens. Changing them takes a click, not a software release.

Database backups

Under System, a screen lets an owner download the entire database as a file, or clear individual tables. It is held behind a permission that no role has by default — only the Super Administrator can reach it — and every download and deletion is recorded.

No undo

Clearing a table cannot be reversed and no table is excluded from the list. Deleting requires typing the word DELETE to confirm. Always take a backup first.

10 Staff and permissions

Who can do what, and who decides.

Staff roles are not fixed in the software. An administrator creates a role, ticks the permissions it should carry, and assigns staff to it. Seven roles ship ready to use and cannot be renamed or deleted, because other parts of the system rely on them existing.

RoleBuilt for
Super AdministratorEverything, including database backups. Hold this closely.
AdministratorDay-to-day running of the marketplace
Support AgentTickets, returns queue, customer lookup
Logistics PersonnelDelivery status only — nothing else
Finance OfficerReconciliation, refunds, payouts
Vendor / CustomerAccount types rather than staff jobs

Two safeguards worth knowing

  • Nobody can grant what they do not have. A staff member building a role can only tick permissions they hold themselves. This stops a limited account from promoting itself.
  • Sensitive actions are split. Working the returns desk and paying refunds are different permissions. So are reviewing partners and paying them. One person should not be able to both approve a payment and make it.

11 Payments that never get lost

The single most expensive failure in any payment system is charging somebody twice for something they already paid for. Here is how that is prevented.

Normally Paystack notifies FirstMaket the moment a payment succeeds, and the plan or order is credited immediately. But that notification can be lost — a network fault, a firewall, or the site restarting at the wrong moment. When it is lost, the customer's money has moved and FirstMaket's records say it has not.

Two safety nets

  • When the customer tries again. Before anybody is sent to pay a second time, FirstMaket asks Paystack directly about their earlier attempts. If one of them actually succeeded, it is credited there and then, and the customer is shown their receipt instead of being charged again.
  • Every fifteen minutes, automatically. The same check runs on a schedule for everyone. A customer who paid, saw nothing happen, and gave up is still credited without ever contacting support.
What Paystack saysWhat FirstMaket does
It succeededCredits it immediately. The record is kept permanently.
It failed or was abandonedRemoves the record — it can never become money.
Still processingNothing at all. The bank has not finished.
Paystack unreachableNothing at all.
The rule that must never bend

If Paystack cannot be reached, nothing is deleted. A timeout tells us nothing, and treating “no answer” as “it failed” is how a business destroys the evidence that a customer paid it. This is enforced in the software and pinned by a test.

This is also why the payment records do not grow without limit: only attempts that Paystack has definitively confirmed as dead are cleared away, and everything that ever became money is kept forever.

13 Running it in production

For whoever operates the servers.

FirstMaket runs on Microsoft Azure App Service with an Azure Database for MySQL. Uploaded files live on a persistent Azure Storage mount that survives deployments.

Automatic background work

Eight jobs run on their own schedule. They are part of the application, so nothing needs to be configured in Azure — deploying the software is enough.

FrequencyJobPurpose
every 15 minReconcile paymentsCredit payments whose notification was lost
hourlyAuto-confirm ordersClose out delivered orders after the grace period
hourlyFlag late preparationSurface vendors sitting on orders
hourlyRevoke unpaid plansRelease prices frozen by plans nobody ever paid

02:30 dailyRisk reviewRaise anomalies for a human to check
One operational constraint

The scheduler and the background worker run one copy per server. If the site is ever scaled out to multiple servers, every one of them would run the schedule and jobs would happen twice. Scale the server up instead, or move the scheduler onto a dedicated instance first.
